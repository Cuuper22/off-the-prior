Off the Prior

Where does a forecaster raised on fake data stop working?

I'm Cúper. In October 2026 Mistral released Timestral, a family of forecasting models that learned mostly from made-up series: trends, repeating seasons and noise, mixed together. It still tops the leaderboards built from real data. A model raised on a made-up world carries a picture of what series look like, its prior. I went looking for the edges of that picture. Hover or tap any underlined word for a quick explanation.

What does Timestral actually do?

You hand it the history of a number and it hands back a forecast, not as one line but as 21 guesses per future step, its quantiles. The middle one is its best bet; the outer ones say how wrong it might be. A forecaster that is honest about its doubt is worth more than one that is confidently wrong, which is also true of people.

How do you tell a failure from bad luck?

A big error is not automatically a failure: some series can't be predicted by anyone. So every test series here comes from a simulator whose exact equations and hidden state I know. Running that simulator forward 500 times from the moment the history ends gives the best forecast anybody could possibly make. I call it the referee. A failure is Timestral being much worse than the referee, measured as an error ratio using MASE. I also compare with three old-school methods.

On home turf (series like its training data) every size makes 0.99–1.15× the referee's error up to 128 steps ahead. It knows this world.

Smooth chaos is where it falls off

The biggest gaps are on smooth, almost-repeating chaotic systems. On the Mackey-Glass delay equation at τ = 30, the smallest model makes 21.87× the referee's error and the largest 13.68×. Coupled Kuramoto oscillators near the edge of syncing: 9.45× at 209M. Bigger models close part of the gap, not most of it.

None of this makes Timestral bad at chaos in absolute terms: against the best old-school method its median ratio is 0.36 at 209M, so it still wins easily. It just leaves a lot on the table that the equations say is there to take.

For Lorenz, the gap lives in the first few Lyapunov times

The famous butterfly system tells a cleaner story. Measure how far ahead a forecast reaches in Lyapunov times and four different sampling speeds land on one curve: the gap peaks a couple of Lyapunov times out and is gone by about ten, where nobody can forecast anything and the referee is reduced to shrugging.

Noise hides the gap

Add a little noise and the gap shrinks fast. Mackey-Glass at 0.1% noise: 43.93–75.47× the referee. At 10% noise: 1.46–1.81×. Real data is noisy, which may be part of why benchmarks built from it never caught this.

Fresh switches get noticed, then ignored

When the rules of a series change (a regime switch), the first steps after the change are the hard part. Sixteen steps after a level jump, the error is 3.77× the referee's at 5M and 2.43× at 209M. A thousand steps after: 1.06× and 1.02×, back to normal.

Does a bigger model close the gap?

The same ratios against model size, both axes logarithmic. On Mackey-Glass at τ = 30 the ratio goes from 21.87× at 5M to 13.68× at 209M; on Kuramoto near the edge of syncing from 12.74× to 9.45×. Sixteen steps after a level jump it goes from 3.77× to 2.43×. Size helps in each of these; switch to the average view to see whether it helps across a generator's whole range of settings.

It knows, it just doesn't use it

So I looked inside. A linear readout of the model's own forecast token predicts the new level after a jump with R² 0.956–0.980, while the forecast itself only gets 0.570–0.726. Its attention even piles onto the patches after the change (15.70–17.69× their fair share). The information is in there. It doesn't make it out.

Wild outliers and long memory stay close

I expected heavy tails to be a disaster. They weren't: wild jumps, long memory, bursts and multifractal cascades all land at 0.94–1.34× the referee. Being surprised in the boring direction still counts as being surprised.

Helper series help only when the link is immediate

Timestral accepts helper series. When a driver sets the main series after a lag, a perfect user of the driver would cut the error several times over. With the driver's future handed over, 28M's gain falls from 4.39 at lag 0 to 0.95 at lag 512, while the referee's stays at 5.62–7.91. Routing the helper both ways instead of one way barely matters: the joint-versus-one-way ratio stays within 5% in 95% of cases. The model mostly isn't using the information, whichever way it arrives.

Does any of this show up on real data?

Real data has no referee, so it can't show a gap to the best possible forecast. What it can show is the pattern against old-school methods. On standard real benchmark series Timestral's error relative to "repeat the last season" is 0.70 at 5M and 0.65 at 209M. On real look-alikes of the failure families (sunspots, lynx, grid frequency, river floods, economic breaks) its lead over old-school methods is smaller than on the synthetic versions.

A fix with no training

A nearest-neighbour method of analogues (find past moments like now, see what came next) beats every Timestral size on the delay and smooth-chaos systems. So let each series decide: backtest both on the end of its own history and switch only when the analogues win clearly. I froze the rule, then tested it on fresh series: chaos errors drop to 0.824× (5M) and 0.982× (209M) of before, while home-turf and regime series are untouched and tails move by at most 1.003×.

The benchmarks

All of this is packaged as two benchmarks with the referee's forecast included: a tagged diagnostic set (372 series, each labelled with why it's hard) and a leaderboard set (3,776 series). On the diagnostic set Timestral-209M makes 2.00× the referee's error and "repeat last season" makes 3.68×. One command scores any model.

Letting a computer hunt for failures

Instead of me picking hard cases, three search methods tweak a series generator to make Timestral miss as badly as possible, each with the same budget of model calls. Left alone, the searches drift toward nearly noise-free series. The biggest scores, in the hundreds, are series where the referee is almost exact while Timestral's own error is ordinary, often better than "repeat last season": the noise effect from earlier, rediscovered by a machine. Keeping only series where the referee's error is at least 5% of "repeat last season"'s, the best failures found are about 17× the referee's error for CMA-ES, 13× for MAP-Elites and 9–11× for gradient search (median of three runs). Gradient search finds the single most extreme cases; MAP-Elites finds the widest spread (21.0 of 40 kinds of failure at 3× or more). One more thing: a tiny, carefully chosen nudge to the history (about 0.034 of its spread per step) doubles Timestral's error, while random nudges of the same size change it by almost nothing.

Teaching it the hard cases

The obvious fix is fine-tuning on the families it fails, tested on families and settings it never trained on, and checked for damage on normal series. This section fills in when those runs finish.

Inside the neurons

Timestral's layers are SwiGLU, so a neuron's value can be positive or negative, and I study each sign as its own entry, a pole: 8,192 of them at 5M and 98,304 at 209M. Every pole is scored against the variables I know exactly because I generated the series: local level, slope, noise, season phase, steps since a switch, which generator made the series. Against this variable library, few poles are single-variable. At θ = 0.7 the share of poles with one clear job is 4.5% at 5M and 2.3% at 209M, and most poles are mixed (83% and 81%).

That number depends on the corpus as much as on the neurons. Scored the same way on corpora with shorter and longer histories, the one-job share is 24–47%, and only 12–55% of poles keep the label they had on the main corpus. Those corpora also hold fewer series, so I reran 5M with controls. Restricting the library to the variables that all corpora define gives 4.8% (from 4.8%), and random libraries of 10 to 50 variables give 3.2–7.2%. Cutting the main corpus down to 1,034 series gives 11–12%, and to 48 series 26–37%: with fewer series the cross-validated ceiling shrinks and single-variable ratios rise. Series count isn't the whole story, though: the short corpus (1,034 series of short histories) gives 41%, far above the main corpus cut to the same number of series, so history length and how the corpus was built matter too. The main-corpus value, from 3,100 series, was still falling as series were added, so it is an upper bound on what a larger corpus would give.

So I read the share as a property of the pair (neurons, variable library, corpus), and I don't take it as evidence that the neurons are polysemantic in general; a different set of labels could give a different answer. Among the poles that do have one job, the label group "position" (where the token sits) covers 59% at 5M and 23% at 209M, and chaos-related labels grow from 1% to 29%.

I also scaled single poles up and down during the forecast and compared the result with predictions written down beforehand. None of the 203 labeled poles matched, and no single pole moved the forecast by more than 0.041 standard deviations. Does that mean anything? To find out I pushed the model along a direction that I know encodes the local level and checked whether the test notices. At 5M and 28M it does (3 and 2 of 18 tries pass), so there the single-pole null result is real. At 123M and 209M it doesn't: pushing the level barely moves their forecasts, so for the big models the single-pole test says nothing either way. Steering whole groups of same-label poles does better than random groups of the same size, mostly by amounts below my original thresholds.

I also tried labels that don't come from my variable list: each pole's favourite patches sorted into 16 shape clusters (the naming rule calls 12 of them "oscillation", so I just number them). I had predicted that small corpora would show tighter shapes. They don't: with fewer series the shapes get looser while the one-job share goes up, which is one more sign that the share is set by the corpus, not by more specialised neurons.

The same goes for the failure link: zeroing the 24 periodic poles that are more active on failing chaos systems leaves 209M's MASE on those systems at 2.05 (from 2.05), and so does zeroing as many random poles (2.05). Open the neuron browser to look at any pole yourself.
